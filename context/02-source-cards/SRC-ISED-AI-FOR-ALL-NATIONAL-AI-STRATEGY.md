## Source-backed facts

**Vision and six pillars**

- The strategy's stated vision is "AI for All," with trust as "the north star," and a framing that "prosperity and sovereignty in this era belong to nations that can leverage trust to adopt, build, and govern AI on their own terms."
- The strategy sets out six pillars organized under three values — Trust, Opportunity, Sovereignty — linked by "Adoption":
  1. Protecting Canadians and safeguarding democracy
  2. Empowering Canadians
  3. Powering AI adoption for shared prosperity
  4. Building the Canadian sovereign AI foundation
  5. Scaling Canadian champions
  6. Building trusted partnerships and global alliances

**Priority sectors**

- The strategy names five priority sectors: health and life sciences; energy and natural resources; transportation; agriculture; manufacturing and robotics.
- Health and life sciences is described as: "World-class research institutions, a universal health system, and a fast-growing life sciences sector position Canada to lead in applying AI to improve health outcomes and deliver faster, more responsive health care services for Canadians."

**AI Missions Program and health funding**

- The strategy will "launch a new AI Missions Program to advance targeted, high-impact projects that deliver significant public good," with the first mission committing "$200 million towards improving health outcomes for Canadians."
- The text states: "Canada's first mission will be in healthcare," citing "a universal public system generating data at a scale most countries cannot match, world-class clinical and research institutions, and a population whose daily lives are touched by the system's pressures." Goals named for this mission: expand access to primary care, reduce ER wait times, prevent avoidable visits through better upstream care, lighten administrative burden on physicians.
- "Subsequent missions will expand to other priority sectors with targets and sequencing refined as implementation develops."

**Health-data infrastructure investments**

- "Canada will invest $100 million to launch the Health Sector Data Space, in partnership with the Canadian Institute for Health Information (CIHI), to link secure, private and standardized datasets to strengthen clinical trials, health services research and performance measurements."
- "Canada will also invest $100 million to expand VITAL in five additional provinces, supporting its ability to leverage clinical data from hospitals and enable health data innovation that reduces mortality rates and accelerates critical care for Canadians."
- VITAL is described as "a pan-Canadian health data platform designed to connect clinical data from hospitals across multiple provinces," already running AI applications "from predicting heart disease to detecting sepsis," with "more than 80 Canadian companies... working with health data and AI to improve triaging, remote monitoring, and diagnostics."
- The strategy states data more broadly will be treated "as a strategic national asset," with healthcare named as "the first focus" for secure government data platforms because "every Canadian relies on the system, and every Canadian stands to benefit."

**Health-AI examples cited in the text**

- CHARTWatch (St. Michael's Hospital, Toronto): an AI system that "continuously monitors patient data, including blood pressure and heart rate, and warns nurses and doctors hours before a patient's condition is about to take a dangerous turn." A study in the Canadian Medical Association Journal found "unexpected deaths on the ward dropped by 26 percent" after deployment.
- Amii (Edmonton) / Okaki: Dr. Ross Mitchell supports Okaki, "a company delivering AI-enabled public health services to Indigenous communities and electronic medical records for all First Nations in the province" (Alberta).
- Amii / Jenkins: Dr. Mitchell also created Jenkins, "an open-source AI scribe now used by hundreds of emergency room physicians across Alberta," associated with physicians "seeing up to 20 percent more patients per shift," with plans to scale it across the country.
- Vector Institute (Toronto): Professor Parvin Mousavi applies machine learning to ultrasound and robotic data for earlier cancer/stroke detection and real-time surgical decision support, including breast cancer and prostate cancer applications.
- A pediatric cardiologist in Halifax, Dr. Robert Chen, is cited in the minister's message as building an AI application to diagnose heart murmurs in newborns.

**Sovereign AI foundation (build-partner-buy)**

- Pillar 4 states sovereignty "depends on three foundations: the compute, cloud, and connectivity that turn ideas into systems; the data that fuels them; and the talent that creates and governs them."
- "To mitigate these dependencies, Canada will adopt a 'build-partner-buy' approach to building its AI infrastructure... building its key sovereign capabilities domestically whenever possible, while partnering with trusted allies or buying existing market solutions when appropriate."
- Key actions include: building "a world-leading public supercomputer"; expanding sovereign compute/cloud capacity (partnerships proposing 850 MW of compute capacity by 2030, scaling to up to 2.3 GW); expanding high-capacity fibre and satellite connectivity; enhancing chip design/fabrication capability; investing in "secure digital systems used for Government operations, including sovereign cloud, AI, cyber and quantum initiatives."
- More than $2 billion in existing investments in Canadian AI compute capacity are referenced, including via the "AI Compute Challenge."
- Canada will provide Canadian SMEs "an additional $700 million in affordable sovereign compute through an expansion of the Compute Access Fund."

**Public service delivery pillar**

- Pillar 3 includes a "Transforming Public Service Delivery with AI" section: "Canada will lead by example in responsible AI use within the public sector," modernizing "how government identifies, procures, and deploys AI tools, ensuring they meet high standards for transparency, privacy, and accountability."
- The text commits to "maintaining a 'human-in-the-loop' approach to ensure strong oversight of meaningful decisions and tasks," while AI is used "to support public servants by reducing administrative burden and enabling better service delivery."
- Key action: "Canada will accelerate the procurement and delivery of AI solutions across the federal government through the Office of Digital Transformation."
- Key action: "Canada will launch the Prime Minister's Innovation Fellows Program to recruit and deploy technical talent" to build internal capacity to "procure, evaluate, and deploy AI systems effectively within government."

**AI safety / governance actions**

- "Canada will invest $50 million to expand the capabilities of the Canadian AI Safety Institute to track emerging AI risks, advance technical research, and to conduct transparent evaluations of AI models."
- "Canada will work on AI transparency, including capabilities like watermarking of AI-generated content."
- "Canada will create a Canada Trusted AI Certification program to help Canadians identify trustworthy AI products in the marketplace."
- "Canada will renew funding for the Standards Council of Canada's AI Program."
- On privacy: "For the Government's own use of personal information, the Government will continue its review of the Privacy Act to meets the needs of Canadians in the digital age," including "considerations around transparency, privacy, and alignment with international standards."
- Other Pillar 1 actions: modernizing consumer privacy legislation; introducing online safety laws; protecting elections/democratic institutions from AI-enabled misinformation and foreign interference.

**Scope/identity note**

- The strategy text does not mention Health Canada or the Public Health Agency of Canada anywhere, by name or abbreviation. "Health and life sciences" and "healthcare" are referenced only as a national priority sector / mission area, not tied to a specific federal department.

## Working interpretation

This strategy is national-level policy framing, not departmental direction — but it is directly useful as context for HC/PHAC's AI document factory because it names the same themes HC/PHAC's internal work already engages with: sovereign compute and data control (echoing INTEL-004's PATH/HAIL control-plane framing), health-sector data platforms (echoing INTEL-002's AI readiness/data foundation theme), human-in-the-loop and transparency/accountability commitments for public-sector AI (relevant to existing PATH/HAIL/CANChat governance work), and a national AI Mission explicitly aimed at health outcomes that, if realized, would likely intersect with federal health-data infrastructure (CIHI, VITAL) that HC/PHAC may interact with. None of this should be read as confirmation that HC/PHAC specifically will receive AI Mission funding, participate in the Health Sector Data Space, or be the delivery vehicle for any pillar — that is interpretation layered on top of a strategy that speaks in terms of "Canadians," "the health system," and named non-HC/PHAC institutions (CIHI, St. Michael's Hospital, Amii, Vector Institute), not HC or PHAC.

## Evidence gaps

- This is a Government of Canada national strategy document, not an HC/PHAC-specific document. It does not name Health Canada or PHAC anywhere in the text.
- The strategy does not state that HC/PHAC will receive AI Mission funding. The first AI Mission's $200 million commitment is described as "towards improving health outcomes for Canadians" generally — no department is named as recipient or delivery lead.
- The strategy does not state that HC/PHAC will participate in the Health Sector Data Space ($100M with CIHI) or in VITAL's expansion ($100M). The named partner for the Health Sector Data Space is CIHI; VITAL is described as a "pan-Canadian" platform connecting "clinical data from hospitals," with no department named as a participant or steward.
- "Health and life sciences" is named as a priority *sector* for the strategy as a whole, not as a commitment to any specific federal health department. This distinction should not be collapsed in downstream intel or pipeline artifacts.
- The health-AI examples cited (CHARTWatch, Okaki, Jenkins, Vector Institute research) are all provincial/institutional/academic implementations — none are federal government deployments, and none involve HC or PHAC.
